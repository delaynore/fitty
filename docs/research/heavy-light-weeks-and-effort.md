# Heavy and light weeks at the same working weight

A lifter can keep the weight on the bar and make a session easier by ending each set earlier. For example, if ten good repetitions are possible at 60 kg today, stopping at five leaves about five repetitions in reserve (RIR). With the same number of sets, this also halves the total repetitions. The example assumes comparable technique, rest and capacity on the day. [Morán-Navarro et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28965198/)

## What changes in a light week?

- **Weekly undulating training** can vary the working weight and repetition target together. In one nine-week trial, 28 participants followed linear, daily undulating or weekly undulating schedules with matched overall volume and intensity. The researchers called an eight-repetition maximum “light” and a four-repetition maximum “heavy.” Both were demanding weights for their assigned rep counts. The trial detected no difference between groups in bench press or leg press maximal strength. [Buford et al., 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18076234/)
- **A deload** has a recovery purpose. In interviews and a Delphi study, strength and physique coaches described deliberately lowering training stress so the athlete can recover for the next training block. They described several ways to do this, including fewer repetitions per set, fewer sets, lighter weights and ending sets farther from failure. These reports document coaching practice. [Bell et al., coach interviews](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9811819/) · [Bell et al., Delphi study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10511399/)

## Should light-week weight increase when heavy-week weight increases?

In eight weeks of squat and bench press training, 21 trained men gained strength using either a percentage of their tested one-repetition maximum (1RM) or weights they chose to reach a target effort. The trial detected no significant difference in strength gains. In a separate experiment, participants could do more repetitions to failure in the squat than in the bench press at 60% of each lift’s 1RM. A percentage of the heavy week’s working weight uses a different reference. Neither study tested how much to raise a light-week target after a lifter adds weight in the heavy week. For Fitty, independent targets per exercise let the lifter choose a weight that suits the light week’s intended sets, repetitions and effort. [Helms et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29628895/) · [Shimano et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17194239/)

## Fatigue, strength and muscle growth

- **Fatigue after a session.** Trained men performed bench presses and squats using a ten-repetition-maximum load. Three sets of ten caused a larger immediate decline in jump height and lifting speed than six sets of five, even though both sessions totaled 30 repetitions per exercise. Performance also recovered faster after the five-repetition sets. This experiment followed recovery for 72 hours. [Morán-Navarro et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28965198/)
- **Strength and growth over eight weeks.** In a squat trial with 22 men, one group stopped sets when repetition speed dropped by 20% and the other stopped at a 40% drop. Stopping earlier meant 40% fewer repetitions with similar squat strength gains. Both groups increased quadriceps volume. Two quadriceps muscles grew more in the longer-set group. In a separate study of 18 trained adults, stopping leg presses and leg extensions at one or two RIR produced similar average quadriceps thickness gains to reaching failure, with less acute fatigue. The size of the reduction in effort matters when applying these results. [Pareja-Blanco et al., 2017](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/sms.12678) · [Refalo et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393985/)
- **Varying effort over several weeks.** In a ten-week trial, 31 trained adults finished either two five-week blocks progressing from four RIR to one RIR, or a program targeting one RIR throughout. The varying-effort group rated sessions easier. Researchers detected no between-group differences in changes in strength or muscle size. How an exact every-other-week reduction at a fixed weight compares with steady training over the long term remains open. [Martikainen et al., 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42978-025-00338-8)
