# Deloads and light or heavy weeks

In strength training, "light and heavy weeks" usually refers to three distinct practices: periodic deload weeks to manage accumulated fatigue, alternating heavy and light days or weeks (undulating periodization), and day-to-day adjustments based on how the lifter feels. This note reviews what primary research supports for healthy adults and how each practice interacts with Fitty's training programs, training days, session targets, and set suggestions.

## What the evidence supports

- **Three completed workouts in seven days establish frequency alone.** In an eight-week trial, all three groups trained three times per week, but performed one, three, or five sets per exercise at each workout. In a separate experiment, trained men recovered lifting speed faster after six sets of five than after three sets of ten to failure at the same weight, even though they completed 30 repetitions in both sessions. Neither study tested a session-count threshold for a deload. Fitty can report the count, but inferring accumulated fatigue or a need for a lighter week requires more information. Coaches in a Delphi study considered how the athlete felt and how they were performing when deciding whether to deload. [Schoenfeld and colleagues, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30153194/) · [Morán-Navarro and colleagues, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28965198/) · [Bell and colleagues, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10511399/)
- **Deloads reduce volume and effort rather than following one fixed formula.** In an international three-round Delphi consensus of strength and physique coaches, experts defined deloading as a temporary reduction in training stress to mitigate physiological and psychological fatigue and restore preparedness. They agreed on reducing training volume—mainly fewer working sets or fewer repetitions per set—and lowering proximity to failure (more repetitions in reserve) or load, while usually keeping exercise selection and weekly frequency intact. Coaches use both pre-planned (proactive) deloads and fatigue-triggered (reactive) deloads, with no single universal percentage drop. [Bell and colleagues, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10511399/) · [Bell and colleagues, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9811819/)
- **In practice, experienced lifters deload roughly every 4–6 weeks for about a week.** A cross-sectional survey of 246 competitive strength and physique athletes (averaging 8.2 years of training experience) reported a mean deload frequency of every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks and a mean duration of 6.4 ± 1.7 days. Athletes most often reduced sets, repetitions, load, and effort while keeping exercise selection and session frequency largely unchanged. Because this is a survey of competitive athletes, it describes common practice rather than proving that every recreational lifter needs scheduled deloads. [Rogerson and colleagues, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10948666/)
- **Short-term trials do not show an automatic bonus from scheduled deloads.** In a 9-week randomized trial of 39 resistance-trained adults, taking a full 1-week break at week 5 produced similar lower-body hypertrophy, power, and endurance to continuous training, but smaller gains in isometric and dynamic lower-body strength. In an 8-week randomized within-subject trial of untrained young men, structured partial deloads (reduced weekly set volume and frequency) matched continuous training for hypertrophy and strength endurance without an extra advantage. Scheduled deloads are therefore a practical tool for managing cumulative fatigue in demanding, longer blocks rather than a mandatory rule for all programs. [Coleman and colleagues, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10809978/) · [Pancar and colleagues, 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13031491/)
- **Complex periodization is optional for general strength and hypertrophy, with a modest strength edge for trained lifters.** The 2026 American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) umbrella review of 137 systematic reviews (over 30,000 participants) found that structured periodization did not consistently outperform non-periodized training across primary outcomes; progressive loading, heavier loads (at least 80% of 1RM) for maximal strength, and sufficient weekly volume (at least 10 sets per muscle group) for hypertrophy mattered more. In volume-equated meta-analyses, periodized training produced slightly greater 1RM strength gains than non-periodized training—with undulating models (rotating heavier and lighter sessions or weeks) modestly favoring linear models in trained lifters—while muscle hypertrophy was essentially identical across linear and daily undulating programs (*d* = −0.02). Earlier ACSM guidance likewise treats undulating heavy/light loading primarily as a tool for intermediate and advanced progression over longer timelines. [ACSM position stand, 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/) · [Moesgaard and colleagues, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35138578/) · [Harries and colleagues, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25611757/) · [Grgic and colleagues, 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5571788/) · [ACSM position stand, 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)
- **Adjusting load or volume to daily readiness works as well as fixed percentages.** A systematic review and meta-analysis of 15 studies in resistance-trained adults found no significant differences in maximal strength or hypertrophy between autoregulated loading (such as adjusting weight via repetitions in reserve or perceived exertion) and fixed percentage-based prescriptions. Letting a lifter dial a single session down on a fatigued day—or push a bit harder on a fresh day—is evidence-consistent without requiring rigid multi-week math. [Hickmott and colleagues, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8762534/)

## How Fitty could apply this

The evidence points away from rigid, one-size-fits-all percentages and toward flexible fatigue management across two complementary layers—planned cycles and day-of readiness:

1. **Support lightweight planned cycles without forcing a strict calendar.** Lifters who want alternating heavy/light weeks or periodic deload weeks (typically 5–7 days every 4–6 weeks) benefit from a way to mark or schedule lighter cycles at the `training program` level—for example, rotating heavy/light weeks or triggering a deload week—while still letting missed gym days slip without breaking a rigid calendar. Because coaches agree on reducing volume (sets/reps) and effort/load rather than enforcing one universal formula, any light/deload mode should offer sensible defaults or light guidance that the lifter can override. [Bell and colleagues, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10511399/) · [Rogerson and colleagues, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10948666/)
2. **Support reactive "light workout today" adjustments for low readiness.** Both qualitative coaching data and autoregulation meta-analyses show that dialing a session down when fatigued restores motivation and readiness without hurting long-term outcomes. Letting a lifter switch an individual `workout session` into a lighter mode (before or during the workout)—reducing planned sets or effort without editing the underlying `training program`—directly serves days when fatigue or low energy hits. [Bell and colleagues, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9811819/) · [Hickmott and colleagues, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8762534/)
3. **Model recurring heavy and light days cleanly inside a program.** Daily undulating layouts (such as `Upper Heavy` at 4 × 5 and `Upper Light` at 3 × 10) already map well to distinct `training days` rotating in program order. A quick **Duplicate day** (or duplicate program) action in the program editor also makes setting up heavy/light or deload variations fast. 4. **Protect `Set suggestion` from cross-talk and deload pollution.** Currently, the first set of an exercise suggests the first logged set from the most recent completed workout that included that exercise, regardless of training day or session intent. Supporting heavy/light weeks and deloads exposes two concrete lookup edge cases that the design must resolve:
   - *Alternating heavy and light days or weeks for the same exercise:* If a lifter alternates heavy (80 kg × 5) and light (60 kg × 10) sessions on the same exercise, pulling from the immediately preceding workout feeds the opposite load into set 1. Scoping suggestions to matching day/week intensity (or the same `training day` first, with a clear fallback) avoids mismatched placeholders.
   - *Deload or low-readiness sessions:* Logging a lighter recovery session should stay visible and clearly labeled in `workout history` without overwriting the normal working-weight `Set suggestion` for the next regular workout.
5. **Keep `Session target` focused where a target drives the UI.** On duration exercises, `Session target` (`±5 s`) directly tells the countdown timer when to signal. For weight-and-repetitions exercises, any session-level lightening should feel just as low-friction—for instance, adjusting planned sets or load guidance at the session level rather than crowding every set row.
